ICASSP 2027 · submission

Label-free is not
zero-shot.

A published AI-music detector reports AUC 0.94 on SONICS without ever seeing a fake label. Take the generated audio out of its dictionary and it does not merely weaken — it inverts. About fifty tracks in six thousand four hundred is the whole difference.

So we propose a detector that never pays that price, because it learns no dictionary at all — and it is the only score we tested that is correctly oriented on all ten generator families, and it needs no random draw anywhere.

What we propose · a detector with no fit set

Tell the detector where to look, and it needs no examples

A dictionary has to discover that periodicity is the signal, and discovery is what costs unlabelled examples — that is the dose the rest of this page measures. But the comb's spacing is f_s / ∏ strides, an architecture constant, and published decoders' stride products form a short list. A detector told where to look learns nothing, so there is no fitting corpus and no dose.

The statistic. Each track becomes a time-averaged log power spectrum with a five-bin running median subtracted, which removes the spectral envelope and leaves the narrow peaks. Call that residual e, above 1 kHz. By the Wiener–Khinchin identity its autocorrelation along frequency is the inverse transform of its power spectrum, so a comb of spacing Δ shows up as a peak at lag Δ/b for bin width b. We use a median rather than the clipped hull the published descriptor uses, because the hull saturates teeth above 5 dB and an autocorrelation peak needs their amplitude.

The prior. Restrict the search to the handful of spacings a real decoder could produce — the lags where a published decoder could put a tooth — instead of taking the largest of ~4,000 noisy candidates. Restriction alone helps, but the restricted score still inherits each track's residual statistics, and Udio's residual is flatter than real music's, which inverts it.

The null. So subtract a null read on the same residual and score the difference. Which null is the detector. We take the prior's own hypothesis space — every lag an admissible fundamental could reach — less a one-bin neighbourhood of every integer multiple of every candidate. Because the null is the alternative hypothesis rather than an arbitrary band, the subtraction is a test and not an offset, and the two sets are disjoint by construction. Deleting only the prior's own lags is not enough: a decoder emits energy at every multiple, so a null holding the comb's higher teeth makes the score subtract its own evidence, scoring −0.128 on a clean synthetic 50 Hz comb where the protected null scores +0.511.

Settings. The prior holds the first M multiples of six published fundamentals spanning 21.5–100 Hz above a 40 Hz floor, and the null spans 40 to M×150 Hz, 150 Hz being the highest plausible decoder fundamental. At M = 4 that leaves 441 admissible lags on FakeMusicCaps and 253 on SONICS. Nothing here is delicate: M ∈ {2, 4} moves macro AUC by at most 0.007 on either corpus, and the 40 Hz floor is not load-bearing either — lowering it to 20 Hz moves macro AUC by −0.006 and +0.000. The one setting that matters is M = 1, which costs 0.015 and 0.029 because Stable Audio's 21.5 Hz series is then unrepresentable above the floor.

No training, no dictionary, no fitted parameter, no seed.

What it does not do. Three things, stated here rather than left to the paper's limitations. It assumes a strided transposed-convolution decoder: a synthesiser built without one leaves no comb to find, and this detector will miss it. It is defeated by pitch shifting, which resamples and moves the spacing off all six candidate frequencies, driving the score below chance at 0.4665 — a signed inversion, not a degradation. And it does not detect Udio: three statistics agree Udio leaves no comb inside these 16 kHz benchmarks' band, so what the calibration buys there is a miss rather than an inversion, which is a smaller and different thing.

It also costs almost nothing to run: 378 audio-seconds per second on four CPU cores, within 3% of the uncalibrated comb statistic, against 8.13 M trainable parameters for a learned competitor. Calibration is essentially free because the null is enumerated rather than drawn: it indexes a precomputed autocorrelation instead of recomputing one.

Contribution 1 · the transductive dose

Putting a number on a limitation the authors stated

The method learns a non-negative dictionary over spectral “fakeprints” and scores a track by the peak energy its reconstruction loses under blurring. No fake label enters the estimator. But the dictionary is learned from a matrix that contains the corpus being scored, and that corpus is majority generated audio. Its authors say so, in their own limitations: the method needs “a sufficiently large sample collection for a synthetic class to detect it (otherwise, it is treated as noise and thus as real)”. What no one had supplied is how large. So we varied one thing only: which unlabelled audio the dictionary sees.

On SONICS the transition is a switch, not a ramp, and it is visible only because every cell was run at three seeds. FakeMusicCaps is different: it never inverts, it climbs smoothly, and what changes below about 5% contamination is the spread across draws rather than the regime. One reading of the SONICS switch is an atom budget, twenty atoms being enough to spend one on the decoder comb only once enough generated audio is present; we have not separated that from initialisation and local optima. The seed standard deviation is not noise either way: it peaks at the transition and collapses immediately after. The error bar is the transition width.

Contribution 2 · signal processing

Bandwidth is not the binding constraint — resolution is

Prior work set both public benchmarks aside because they ship at 16 kHz, which cuts off the band the fakeprint was designed for. With the band re-derived for the lower rate the method still reaches 0.9917 and 0.9389, so the cutoff is survivable — and that is what makes every other measurement on this page possible. Their published descriptor transfers essentially unchanged. What binds is coarser: the requirement is a resolution of about 2.7 Hz per bin, not any particular dimension, and it has to be re-derived when the sample rate changes.

The mechanism · checked, not asserted

What the atoms encode — and where the evidence runs out

A transposed-convolution decoder leaves a comb whose spacing is the sample rate divided by the product of its strides — an architecture constant, fixed before the model sees any data. So the claim “the dictionary learns decoder combs” is testable: measure each learned atom’s own comb spacing and check it against the spacing measured on the corpus. The test needs a null, and the choice of null decides the answer.

 An atom counts as on a comb if its recovered spacing is within 1% of a measured decoder spacing or its half or quarter. The obvious null — a fundamental drawn uniformly from 40–700 Hz — is too weak, because the atom spacings are themselves selected by the pipeline. The honest null scores each corpus’s atoms against the other corpus’s measured decoder spacing. It splits the result.

On FakeMusicCaps the claim survives, and for an identifiable reason. Six atoms sit on a measured fundamental and none on SONICS’s. The discriminating evidence is MusicGen’s 250.1 Hz cluster against a measured 250.0 — a spacing no other family here shares, recovered from audio carrying no label.

On SONICS it does not. Its comb is at 399.902 Hz and FakeMusicCaps’ at 200.195. Half of 399.902 is 199.95 — 0.12% away. The two corpora’s decoders sit within 0.12% of a 2:1 ratio, so once sub-multiples are admitted, spacing cannot say which decoder a SONICS atom belongs to. We report it as unresolved.

This is one of the claims the project has had to withdraw, and it was caught by running the null a referee would have run. The rule that made the count impressive — admitting sub-multiples — is the same rule that destroyed its power to attribute. What survives on both corpora needs no attribution at all: mean atom periodicity is 0.700 on FakeMusicCaps against 0.464 on SONICS, ordered exactly like the AUCs.

Contribution 3 · deployment and fairness

What the benchmark measures, and what a deployment costs

On the uncontrolled SONICS corpus a single high-frequency floor statistic — nothing but the delivery chain — reaches signed AUC 0.9842, correctly oriented on all five generator families. Matching the chain removes it, and costs our own comb arm 0.217 AUC, which we report against ourselves.

At a 4.7% false-positive rate the dictionary score on SONICS recalls 67.4% of generated tracks, which at a 1% base rate of AI music is a precision of 0.127 — nearly seven false accusations for every correct one. Thresholding on in-corpus real music and applying that threshold to unseen real music moves the false-positive rate from 0.042 to 0.115.

That error is not spread evenly. Across 2,998 tracks the false-positive rate is 0.236 on Electronic and 0.028 on Folk, an 8.5× spread — and the mechanism is the detector’s own, since electronic production already carries quantised spectral structure resembling a decoder comb. A deployed system would systematically over-accuse electronic artists.

The proposed detector can be equalised, and the reason is that no margin is tied at zero: a quantile threshold cannot be placed inside a tie mass. Under group-conditional conformal calibration, all eight genres land between 4.4% and 5.1% against a 5% target — a 1.17× spread. Its threshold also travels: applied to unseen FMA reals it lands under budget at 0.72× the intended rate while catching 87.6% of generated tracks.

One caveat we will not drop. Precision is a property of the base rate, not of a detector, and it is more useful stated as a requirement than as a verdict: on unseen FMA reals, reaching precision 0.9 needs a prevalence near 6% at q = 0.999, or 30% at q = 0.95. At a 1% base rate no operating point we measured gets there. Screening a stream of plays and screening a stream of uploads are different problems, and that is a deployment question rather than a detector question.

Before this · two falsified theses

What we set out to do, and why it failed

The project began by estimating the intrinsic dimension of a track’s embedding cloud, on the theory that real music lives on a higher-dimensional manifold. The sign turned out to be generator-dependent — two generators one way, three the other — so no threshold exists.

It then spent its longest phase on one-class normalising flows: fit a density on real music, flag the unlikely. Six formulations, including every score correction in the out-of-distribution literature, all inverted. A capacity ladder made detection monotonically worse as the density got better, and a held-out likelihood decomposition put the flow at chance.

One mechanism explains both. Generated audio is simpler and more regular than real music, so every statistic of the form “is this unusual for real music?” ranks it as more real. No reweighting of the same likelihood escapes that. The rule this earned: a statistic whose sign is fitted will get its sign wrong on the case you care about — take the sign from the mechanism instead. Which is what a decoder comb provides, and why the third act is about physics rather than statistics.
